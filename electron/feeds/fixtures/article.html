Why the Baltic Sea Is Turning Into a Dead Zone

by Iris Lowell · September 10, 2026

On a windless July morning, the water off the Swedish island of Gotland looks less like a sea than a bowl of pea soup. Streaks of yellow-green cyanobacteria stretch to the horizon, thick enough to smother a paddle. From orbit, the bloom is visible as a marbled swirl the size of a small country. It is beautiful, and it is a symptom of one of the largest human-made dead zones on Earth.

The Baltic is an unusual sea. It is shallow, brackish, and almost entirely enclosed; water entering from the North Sea through the narrow Danish straits takes roughly thirty years to cycle out again. Whatever the nine surrounding countries put into it—fertilizer runoff, sewage, industrial effluent—stays for a generation. Over the past century, that has added up to an enormous surplus of nitrogen and phosphorus, the nutrients that feed the blooms.

When the blooms die and sink, bacteria consume them, and in doing so consume the oxygen in the deep water. Below about 70 metres, much of the central Baltic now contains no oxygen at all. Cod eggs, which need saline, oxygenated water to float, sink into the dead layer and suffocate. The cod fishery, once the region's most valuable, collapsed in 2019 and has not recovered.

A slow recovery

There is, at least, good news on the input side. Since the 1980s, the countries around the Baltic have cut phosphorus loads by roughly half and nitrogen by a third, through better sewage treatment and stricter farming rules. Helsinki's wastewater plant now removes more than 95 percent of the phosphorus that reaches it. Poland, long the largest single source, has built hundreds of treatment plants since joining the European Union.

But the sea is slow to respond. Decades of phosphorus have accumulated in the sediment, and each summer, as the deep water runs out of oxygen, chemical reactions release it back into the water column—what scientists call the “vicious circle” of Baltic eutrophication. Even if every farm and city stopped polluting tomorrow, the dead zone would persist for decades.

“We are not fighting today's pollution. We are fighting the pollution of our grandparents.”

Jacob Carstensen, Aarhus University

Some researchers are looking at more drastic interventions: pumping oxygenated surface water into the depths, or spreading aluminium compounds to bind the phosphorus in the sediment. Small trials in Swedish bays have shown promise. Scaling them up to a sea of 400,000 square kilometres is another matter.

Back on Gotland, the bloom will drift for a few more weeks before the autumn storms break it up. The tourists will go home. And below the surface, in the dark, the sediment will keep a patient ledger of everything the land has sent it.
